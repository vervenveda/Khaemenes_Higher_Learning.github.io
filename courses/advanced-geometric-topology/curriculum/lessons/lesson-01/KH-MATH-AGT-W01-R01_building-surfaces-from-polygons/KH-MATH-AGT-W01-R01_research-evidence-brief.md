## Research question
How can a learner move rigorously from the intuitive act of “gluing polygon edges” to the quotient-space construction of compact surfaces, while correctly distinguishing the polygonal presentation from the resulting topological space?

## Instructional claims to support

### C1 — Quotient construction
Given a space X and an equivalence relation ~, the quotient set X/~ consists of equivalence classes, and the quotient topology is defined so that a subset U of X/~ is open exactly when its inverse image under the quotient map is open in X.

**Course use:** Establishes the formal meaning of identifying points.

### C2 — Polygonal presentation
Compact connected triangulable surfaces can be represented as quotient spaces obtained from a polygonal region by identifying boundary edges in pairs.

**Course use:** Justifies polygon models as more than pictures.

### C3 — Paired edge data
A polygonal presentation records which boundary edges are paired and the orientation of each identification. The edge data determines an equivalence relation on boundary points and, through endpoints, induces vertex equivalence classes.

**Course use:** Enables later computation of V, E, F and orientability.

### C4 — Standard examples
Standard paired-edge presentations produce familiar closed surfaces including the sphere, torus, real projective plane, and Klein bottle.

**Course use:** Gives the learner a controlled family of benchmark constructions.

### C5 — Representation is not the object
The polygon is a presentation of the quotient surface; it is not itself the resulting surface. Different presentations can describe homeomorphic surfaces.

**Course use:** Prevents a major conceptual error before classification.

### C6 — Euler characteristic preparation
Once a polygonal quotient is regarded as a cell decomposition, the counts of vertex, edge, and face equivalence classes prepare the learner to compute χ = V − E + F.

**Course use:** Prepares Week 1 Session 3 and Week 3 invariant work.

### C7 — Experiment is not proof
Physical folding, diagrams, and computational visualization can support intuition and detect patterns, but a topological claim requires an appropriate mathematical argument.

**Course use:** Establishes the course-wide evidence boundary.

## Proof obligations for this reading
This reading does **not** need to prove the full classification theorem. It should:
1. define equivalence relation and quotient space precisely;
2. explain how paired boundary identifications generate the quotient;
3. verify the standard torus construction carefully;
4. give defensible explanations of the sphere, projective-plane, and Klein-bottle models;
5. distinguish local geometric visualization from a proof of homeomorphism;
6. defer the full compact-surface classification theorem to Week 2.

## Misconceptions to address
1. “Gluing” means ordinary bending in three-dimensional space.
2. Every edge-arrow drawing automatically produces a surface.
3. The polygon and the quotient are the same space.
4. Equal edge labels alone specify the gluing without orientation information.
5. Every polygonal presentation is unique.
6. A convincing picture is a proof.
7. Euler characteristic counts the raw polygon vertices and edges before identifications.
8. Orientation of an edge arrow is the same thing as orientability of the resulting surface.
