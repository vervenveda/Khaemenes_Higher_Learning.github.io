Worked Surface Identification Examples

Advanced Geometric Topology · Self-Paced Lesson 03

Resource ID: KH-MATH-AGT-W01-WE01 (legacy stable ID)

Companions: Lesson 01 — Building Surfaces from Polygons · Lesson 02 — Quotient Spaces & Polygonal Schemas

Purpose

This resource teaches a repeatable method for analyzing polygonal presentations. The goal is not to recognize a familiar drawing by sight. The goal is to extract and justify the quotient data.

word → edge pairings → endpoint relations → vertex classes → quotient cells → invariants → justified conclusion

The Identification Audit

  1. Fix the boundary word and orientation convention.
  2. List paired edges.
  3. Translate each edge pairing into endpoint relations.
  4. Take the transitive closure to obtain vertex equivalence classes.
  5. Count quotient cells V, E, and F.
  6. Compute χ=V−E+F.
  7. Inspect the edge pattern for orientation-reversing behavior.
  8. Check that the quotient is a surface when that has not already been established.
  9. State only the conclusion justified by the evidence available at this point in the course.

Example 1 — The Standard Torus Word

Boundary word: a b a−1 b−1.

Label square vertices v0,v1,v2,v3 in boundary order. Under the course convention, the a-pair gives v0~v3 and v1~v2. The b-pair gives v1~v0 and v2~v3. Transitive closure yields one vertex class.

(V,E,F)=(1,2,1), χ=0.

This is the established benchmark torus presentation and is orientable.

Reasoning lesson: quotient cells were derived from the identifications, not from the visual appearance of the square.

Example 2 — The Real Projective Plane

Boundary word: a a.

Use a 2-gon with vertices v0,v1. The same-sign occurrences induce endpoint relations that merge v0 and v1. Hence V=1, E=1, F=1.

χ=1.

This is the established benchmark presentation of RP² and is nonorientable.

Example 3 — A Sphere from a 2-gon

Boundary word: a a−1.

The opposite-sign pairing leaves two quotient vertex classes. Thus V=2, E=1, F=1.

χ=2.

This is the established benchmark presentation of S².

Comparison: Examples 2 and 3 use the same label and the same number of polygon edges, but different directional correspondence produces different endpoint equivalence relations.

Example 4 — The Klein Bottle

Boundary word: a b a b−1.

For the a-pair, the same signs give v0~v2 and v1~v3. For the b-pair, the opposite signs give v1~v0 and v2~v3. All four vertices therefore lie in one equivalence class.

(V,E,F)=(1,2,1), χ=0.

The Klein bottle and torus share χ=0, but the benchmark Klein-bottle presentation is nonorientable whereas the torus is orientable.

Reasoning lesson: equality of one invariant does not imply homeomorphism.

Example 5 — Same Surface, Different Presentation

Consider a b b−1 a−1. The adjacent inverse pair b b−1 is a cancellable feature in the standard polygonal-presentation calculus, leaving the benchmark sphere pattern a a−1.

Thus the presentation again yields S².

Reasoning lesson: polygonal words are presentations, not canonical names.

Example 6 — Genus Two: Do Not Guess from the Octagon

Consider the standard orientable genus-two word

a b a−1 b−1 c d c−1 d−1.

There are four quotient edge classes. The standard endpoint identifications place all eight polygon vertices into one quotient vertex class, and the polygon interior gives one face.

(V,E,F)=(1,4,1), χ=−2.

The word consists of two orientable commutator blocks, the standard genus-two pattern.

Example 7 — Same χ Does Not Mean Same Surface

PresentationVEFχOrientability
a b a⁻¹ b⁻¹1210Orientable
a b a b⁻¹1210Nonorientable

They share χ=0 but are not homeomorphic. Never reverse “different invariant implies different” into “same invariant implies same.”

Example 8 — A Quotient That Requires a Local Check

Suppose boundary material is collapsed or identified in a way not covered by an established paired-edge surface construction. Let p be a suspicious quotient point. Inspect q−1(p) and determine whether a sufficiently small neighborhood of p is locally Euclidean.

If the neighborhood branches, pinches, or otherwise fails the required local model, the quotient is not a surface at p.

Later connection: Arc 02 will turn this local-neighborhood question into a combinatorial audit using stars and links.

Example 9 — Debugging a False Solution

Claim: “The square has four vertices, four edges, and one face, so every square gluing has χ=1.”

Diagnosis: cells were counted before quotient identifications.

Correction: for the torus, four corners become one quotient vertex and four sides become two quotient edges, so χ=1−2+1=0.

Transfer rule: perform identifications first; count quotient cells second.

Example 10 — Evidence Ladder

Suppose an unfamiliar edge word gives a verified cell structure V=1,E=3,F=1, hence χ=−1. At this stage you may report χ, compare with established benchmark invariants, and investigate orientability. You may not invoke a classification theorem that the course has not yet established merely to name the surface.

Reasoning lesson: mathematical maturity includes knowing the boundary of the argument currently available.

Independent Try

  1. For a b a⁻¹ b⁻¹, reconstruct the endpoint relations without copying Example 1.
  2. Explain exactly why a a and a a⁻¹ produce different vertex counts.
  3. For a b a b⁻¹, show that all four square corners lie in one equivalence class.
  4. Compute χ for the genus-two word in Example 6.
  5. Explain why χ(T²)=χ(Klein bottle) does not imply homeomorphism.
  6. Describe a local-neighborhood test for a suspicious quotient vertex.
  7. For each answer, label which steps are direct quotient computations and which use an established benchmark result.

Worked-Example Checklist

  • boundary word and convention;
  • paired-edge list;
  • endpoint relations;
  • vertex equivalence classes;
  • V,E,F;
  • χ calculation;
  • orientation evidence when relevant;
  • local surface check when relevant;
  • conclusion stated no more strongly than the evidence permits.

Accessibility

No example depends on color. Every presentation is encoded as text. Any future diagram must also provide boundary order, traversal direction, edge-pair data, and endpoint correspondence in text. No paid software, physical model, or external learner website is required.
