Worked Surface Identification Examples

Advanced Geometric Topology · Unit 1 · Week 1

Resource ID: KH-MATH-AGT-W01-WE01

Companions: R01 Building Surfaces from Polygons · CR01 Quotient Spaces & Polygonal Schemas

Purpose

This resource teaches a repeatable method for analyzing polygonal presentations. The goal is not to recognize a familiar drawing by sight. The goal is to extract and justify the quotient data.

The Identification Audit

  1. Fix the boundary word and orientation convention.
  2. List paired edges.
  3. Translate each edge pairing into endpoint relations.
  4. Take the transitive closure to obtain vertex equivalence classes.
  5. Count quotient cells V, E, and F.
  6. Compute χ = V − E + F.
  7. Inspect the edge pattern for orientation-reversing behavior.
  8. Check that the quotient is a surface when that has not already been established.
  9. State only the conclusion justified by the evidence available at this point in the course.

Example 1 — The Standard Torus Word

Boundary word: a b a−1 b−1.

Label the square vertices v0,v1,v2,v3 in boundary order. Under our convention, the a-pair matches the endpoints so that v0~v3 and v1~v2. The b-pair gives v1~v0 and v2~v3. Taking transitive closure yields one vertex class:

[v0]=[v1]=[v2]=[v3].

The paired sides give E=2 edge classes, and the polygon interior gives F=1. Therefore

χ = 1 − 2 + 1 = 0.

The standard edge pattern is the familiar orientable torus pattern. At this stage we may identify the quotient with the torus because this is one of our established benchmark presentations.

Reasoning lesson: “It looks like a doughnut” was not used to count the quotient cells.

Example 2 — The Real Projective Plane

Boundary word: a a.

Use a 2-gon model with vertices v0 and v1. Both occurrences of a have the same orientation in the boundary word. The edge identification identifies the two boundary edges and sends endpoints so that v0~v1. Hence V=1.

There is one edge class and one face:

(V,E,F)=(1,1,1), so χ=1.

The same-direction repeated symbol is the standard projective-plane presentation. The resulting surface is nonorientable.

Reasoning lesson: the word a a and a a−1 are not interchangeable. Orientation data changes the quotient.

Example 3 — A Sphere from a 2-gon

Boundary word: a a−1.

Again begin with vertices v0 and v1. This time the paired edge orientations oppose one another in the boundary word. The endpoint correspondence leaves two quotient vertex classes rather than merging them into one.

Thus V=2, E=1, F=1, and

χ = 2 − 1 + 1 = 2.

This is a standard polygonal presentation of S².

Comparison with Example 2: the two examples use the same number of polygon edges. The orientation pattern changes the endpoint equivalence relation and therefore changes the quotient topology.

Example 4 — The Klein Bottle

Boundary word: a b a b−1.

Label the square vertices v0,v1,v2,v3 in boundary order. For the a-pair, the same exponent sign gives endpoint relations v0~v2 and v1~v3. For the b-pair, the opposite signs give v1~v0 and v2~v3. Together these relations put all four vertices into one class.

Hence V=1, E=2, F=1 and

χ = 0.

Notice that this is the same Euler characteristic as the torus. Yet the Klein bottle is nonorientable while the torus is orientable.

Reasoning lesson: Euler characteristic alone does not distinguish these two surfaces. An invariant is useful only to the extent that it separates the objects under consideration.

Example 5 — Same Surface, Different Presentation

Consider the sphere presentation

a b b−1 a−1.

This four-edge word looks more complicated than a a−1, but complexity of the word does not imply a different homeomorphism type. The adjacent pair b b−1 is a cancellable feature in the standard polygonal-presentation calculus; the remaining pattern reduces to a a−1.

Therefore the quotient is again S².

Reasoning lesson: polygonal presentations are not canonical names. Different words can represent homeomorphic surfaces.

Example 6 — Genus Two: Do Not Guess from the Octagon

Consider the standard orientable genus-two word

a b a−1 b−1 c d c−1 d−1.

There are four edge classes: a,b,c,d. The standard endpoint identifications place all eight polygon vertices into one quotient vertex class. The polygon interior contributes one face.

Thus

(V,E,F)=(1,4,1), χ=1−4+1=−2.

The word consists of two orientable commutator blocks, the standard pattern for a closed orientable surface of genus two.

Reasoning lesson: “octagon” is not the topological answer. The edge word is the relevant quotient data.

Example 7 — Same χ Does Not Mean Same Surface

Return to the torus and Klein bottle:

PresentationVEFχOrientability
a b a⁻¹ b⁻¹1210Orientable
a b a b⁻¹1210Nonorientable

They share χ=0 but are not homeomorphic.

Reasoning lesson: a numerical invariant can fail to distinguish two spaces. Never reverse the logic “different invariant implies different” into “same invariant implies same.”

Example 8 — A Quotient That Requires a Local Check

Suppose a polygonal construction is altered so that more boundary material is collapsed or identified in a way not covered by our paired-edge surface theorem. A drawing may still appear surface-like away from the special quotient point.

Do not name the result immediately. Let p be the suspicious quotient point and inspect q−1(p). Ask whether a sufficiently small neighborhood of p is homeomorphic to an open disk. If it branches, pinches, or otherwise fails the local Euclidean condition, the quotient is not a surface at p.

Reasoning lesson: the surface condition is local. Familiar global appearance cannot repair a singular neighborhood.

Example 9 — Debugging a False Solution

Student claim: “The square has four vertices, four edges, and one face, so every square gluing has χ=4−4+1=1.”

Diagnosis: the student counted cells in the polygon before taking the quotient.

Correction: V and E in the quotient cell decomposition are equivalence-class counts. For the standard torus, four corners become one vertex class and four sides become two edge classes, giving χ=1−2+1=0.

Transfer rule: perform identifications first; count quotient cells second.

Example 10 — Evidence Ladder

Suppose you encounter an unfamiliar edge word and obtain V=1, E=3, F=1, hence χ=−1.

What may you conclude immediately?

  • You may report χ=−1 for the verified cell structure.
  • You may compare this value with benchmark surfaces whose invariants are already known.
  • You may investigate orientability.
  • You may not invoke a classification theorem that the course has not yet established merely to name the surface.

Reasoning lesson: mathematical maturity includes knowing the boundary of the argument you currently possess.

Independent Try — Before Looking at Later Solutions

  1. For a b a⁻¹ b⁻¹, reconstruct the endpoint relations without copying Example 1.
  2. Explain exactly why a a and a a⁻¹ produce different vertex counts.
  3. For a b a b⁻¹, show that all four square corners lie in one equivalence class.
  4. Compute χ for the genus-two word in Example 6.
  5. Write one sentence explaining why χ(T²)=χ(Klein bottle) does not imply the spaces are homeomorphic.
  6. Describe a local-neighborhood test you would apply to a suspicious quotient vertex.

Worked-Example Checklist

When you write your own solution, include:

  • [ ] boundary word and convention;
  • [ ] paired-edge list;
  • [ ] endpoint relations;
  • [ ] vertex equivalence classes;
  • [ ] V,E,F;
  • [ ] χ calculation;
  • [ ] orientation evidence when relevant;
  • [ ] local surface check when relevant;
  • [ ] conclusion stated no more strongly than the evidence permits.

Accessibility

No example depends on color. Every presentation is encoded as text. When diagrams are later added, each must include a textual boundary word, traversal direction, edge-pair list, and endpoint correspondence.
