Polygon Gluing & Surface Classification Laboratory

Advanced Geometric Topology · Self-Paced Lesson 05

Resource ID: KH-MATH-AGT-W01-LAB01

Mode: construct → record → compare → conjecture → audit → justify

Laboratory Question

How much information about a quotient surface can be recovered systematically from a polygonal presentation, and where does experimental or combinatorial evidence stop short of proof?

Learning Targets

  • Translate polygonal edge data into endpoint equivalence relations.
  • Construct vertex equivalence classes using transitive closure.
  • Determine quotient cell counts V, E, and F.
  • Compute Euler characteristic from the quotient cell structure.
  • Record orientation evidence without confusing it with a complete classification proof.
  • Compare different polygonal presentations.
  • Identify when a local manifold check is required.
  • Separate observation, computation, conjecture, and proof.
  • Create a reproducible mathematical lab record.

Materials

No paid software or external website is required. You may use paper, pencil, index cards, string/tape, or internal Khaemenes tools when useful. A calculator is optional; the arithmetic is intentionally simple.

Model note: Physical folding is only a model of the identification instructions. Do not force a paper construction to behave like an embedding in three-dimensional space.

Investigation A — Reproduce the Torus Audit

Specimen: a b a−1 b−1

Perform the complete lab record without copying Lesson 03. Your endpoint relations must be explicit.

Required result

Verify from the quotient data that the standard cell structure has V=1, E=2, F=1 and χ=0.

Reproducibility check

Could another learner reconstruct your vertex classes using only your written record? If not, add the missing correspondence data.

Investigation B — Change One Orientation

  • B1: a b a−1 b−1
  • B2: a b a b−1

Keep a side-by-side record of endpoint relations, quotient cell counts, χ, and orientation evidence.

  1. Which recorded quantities remain the same?
  2. Which structural feature changes?
  3. Why does equal χ fail to prove the quotients are homeomorphic?

Investigation C — Same Edge Count, Different Quotient

Compare a a−1 and a a. Track endpoints carefully. Determine V, E, F, and χ for each.

Reflection: Explain why “both diagrams have two polygon edges” contains too little information to determine the quotient surface.

Investigation D — Presentation Complexity

Compare the standard sphere presentation a a−1 with a b b−1 a−1. Document the elementary polygonal-scheme simplification of the adjacent inverse pair.

Question: What does this tell you about using boundary-word length as a topological invariant?

Investigation E — Genus-Two Cell Audit

Specimen: a b a−1 b−1 c d c−1 d−1

  1. Label all eight polygon vertices.
  2. Record every endpoint relation induced by a,b,c,d.
  3. Take transitive closure.
  4. Determine V,E,F.
  5. Compute χ.

Restriction: Do not use χ=2−2g to obtain the answer. The cell structure must generate the computation.

Investigation G — Local Neighborhood Forensics

Consider an unfamiliar quotient construction outside the proper paired-edge patterns studied above. Choose a suspicious quotient point p and:

  1. describe q−1(p);
  2. describe a small neighborhood of that preimage;
  3. determine how neighborhood pieces are identified;
  4. ask whether the resulting neighborhood is homeomorphic to an open disk;
  5. if not, describe the obstruction.

“Insufficient evidence” is an acceptable scholarly conclusion when justified.

Investigation H — Design Your Own Specimen

Create a 4-, 6-, or 8-edge polygonal word in which every edge label appears exactly twice.

  1. State your convention.
  2. Predict V,E,F and χ.
  3. Perform the endpoint-equivalence audit.
  4. Revise your prediction if necessary.
  5. Record orientation evidence.
  6. State what you can and cannot classify using results established so far.

Do not engineer the answer backward from a named surface.

Investigation I — Counterexample Laboratory

  1. “Polygonal presentations with the same number of sides represent the same surface.”
  2. “If two surfaces have the same Euler characteristic, they are homeomorphic.”
  3. “Every quotient obtained by identifying polygon boundary points is a surface.”
  4. “A more complicated boundary word must represent a more complicated surface.”

For each, justify it from established results or give a counterexample/reason it fails. Record which hypothesis or inference breaks.

Investigation J — Reproducibility Exchange

Give your Investigation H record to another learner, or set it aside and reconstruct it later without looking at your diagram. Record any ambiguity and revise the record until the quotient-cell audit is reproducible.

Final Laboratory Report

  • completed records for Investigations A–F;
  • a local-neighborhood forensic plan for G;
  • your original specimen and audit from H;
  • counterexample analysis from I;
  • reproducibility revision from J;
  • a one-paragraph conclusion answering the laboratory question;
  • a section titled What I observed, what I computed, and what I proved.

Optional Internal Tools

The Khaemenes Scientific Calculator may check arithmetic, although it is not needed for core calculations. Evidence & Citation Studio can support an extended sourced investigation. Neither replaces the mathematical record.

Accessibility

No task requires color, three-dimensional vision, or a physical model. Every specimen is represented by a textual boundary word. The entire laboratory can be completed using endpoint relations and written quotient data.
