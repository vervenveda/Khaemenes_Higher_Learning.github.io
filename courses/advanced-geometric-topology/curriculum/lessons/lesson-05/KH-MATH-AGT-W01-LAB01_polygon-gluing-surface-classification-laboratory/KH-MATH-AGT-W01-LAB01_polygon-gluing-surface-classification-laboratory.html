Polygon Gluing & Surface Classification Laboratory

Advanced Geometric Topology · Unit 1 · Week 1 · Lesson 05

Resource ID: KH-MATH-AGT-W01-LAB01

Mode: construct → record → compare → conjecture → audit → justify

Laboratory Question

How much information about a quotient surface can be recovered systematically from a polygonal presentation, and where does experimental or combinatorial evidence stop short of proof?

Learning Targets

  • Translate polygonal edge data into endpoint equivalence relations.
  • Construct vertex equivalence classes using transitive closure.
  • Determine quotient cell counts V, E, and F.
  • Compute Euler characteristic from the quotient cell structure.
  • Record orientation evidence without confusing it with a complete classification proof.
  • Compare different polygonal presentations.
  • Identify when a local manifold check is required.
  • Separate observation, computation, conjecture, and proof.
  • Create a reproducible mathematical lab record.

Materials

No paid software or external website is required. You may use paper, pencil, index cards, string/tape, or the internal Khaemenes tools when useful. A calculator is optional; the arithmetic in this laboratory is intentionally simple.

Safety / model note: Physical folding is only a model of the identification instructions. Do not force paper constructions to behave like embeddings in three-dimensional space.

Evidence Vocabulary

StatusMeaning in this lab

Lab Record Template

For every specimen, record:

  1. boundary word;
  2. edge pairs and directions;
  3. endpoint relations;
  4. vertex equivalence classes;
  5. V, E, F;
  6. χ=V−E+F;
  7. orientation evidence;
  8. local-surface check, if needed;
  9. classification claim, if justified;
  10. evidence status: observation / computation / established theorem / proof.

Investigation A — Reproduce the Torus Audit

Specimen: a b a−1 b−1

Perform the complete lab record without copying WE01. Your endpoint relations must be explicit.

Required result

Verify from the quotient data that the standard cell structure has V=1, E=2, F=1 and χ=0.

Reproducibility check

Could another learner reconstruct your vertex classes using only your written record? If not, add the missing correspondence data.

Investigation B — Change One Orientation

Compare:

  • Specimen B1: a b a−1 b−1
  • Specimen B2: a b a b−1

Keep a side-by-side record of endpoint relations, quotient cell counts, χ, and orientation evidence.

Analysis

  1. Which recorded quantities remain the same?
  2. Which structural feature changes?
  3. Why does equal χ fail to prove the two quotients are homeomorphic?

Investigation C — Same Edge Count, Different Quotient

Compare the 2-gon words:

  • C1: a a−1
  • C2: a a

Track the endpoints carefully. Determine V, E, F, and χ for each.

Reflection

Explain why “both diagrams have two polygon edges” contains too little information to determine the quotient surface.

Investigation D — Presentation Complexity

Compare a standard sphere presentation a a−1 with the expanded presentation a b b−1 a−1.

Document how the adjacent inverse pair can be removed by the elementary polygonal-scheme simplification already introduced in the worked examples.

Question

What does this tell you about using the length of a boundary word as a topological invariant?

Investigation E — Genus-Two Cell Audit

Specimen: a b a−1 b−1 c d c−1 d−1

  1. Label all eight polygon vertices.
  2. Record every endpoint relation induced by a,b,c,d.
  3. Take transitive closure.
  4. Determine V,E,F.
  5. Compute χ.

Restriction: Do not use χ=2−2g to obtain the answer. The cell structure must generate the computation.

Investigation G — Local Neighborhood Forensics

Consider an unfamiliar quotient construction that collapses or identifies boundary data outside the proper paired-edge patterns studied above.

Choose a suspicious quotient point p and write a local forensic plan:

  1. Describe q−1(p).
  2. Describe a small neighborhood of that preimage in the polygon.
  3. Determine how the neighborhood pieces are identified.
  4. Ask whether the resulting neighborhood of p is homeomorphic to an open disk.
  5. If it is not, describe the obstruction: branching, pinching, boundary behavior, or another failure.

This investigation may end with “insufficient evidence.” That is an acceptable scholarly conclusion.

Investigation H — Design Your Own Specimen

Create a 4-, 6-, or 8-edge polygonal word in which every edge label appears exactly twice.

  1. State your convention.
  2. Predict the quotient's V,E,F and χ.
  3. Perform the endpoint-equivalence audit.
  4. Revise your prediction if necessary.
  5. Record orientation evidence.
  6. State what you can and cannot classify using only Week 1 results.

Do not engineer the answer backward from a named surface. The purpose is to investigate the quotient data.

Investigation I — Counterexample Laboratory

Test each proposed rule:

  1. “Polygonal presentations with the same number of sides represent the same surface.”
  2. “If two surfaces have the same Euler characteristic, they are homeomorphic.”
  3. “Every quotient obtained by identifying polygon boundary points is a surface.”
  4. “A more complicated boundary word must represent a more complicated surface.”

For each rule, either justify it from established results or give a counterexample / reason it fails. Record exactly which hypothesis or inference breaks.

Investigation J — Reproducibility Exchange

Give your Investigation H record to another learner, or set it aside and reconstruct it later without looking at your diagram. The record must contain enough information to reproduce the quotient-cell audit.

Record any ambiguity discovered. Revise the lab record so that the mathematical procedure is reproducible.

Final Laboratory Report

Your report must contain:

  • completed records for Investigations A–F;
  • a local-neighborhood forensic plan for G;
  • your original specimen and audit from H;
  • counterexample analysis from I;
  • reproducibility revision from J;
  • a one-paragraph conclusion answering the laboratory question;
  • a separate section titled What I observed, what I computed, and what I proved.

Optional Internal Tools

Accessibility

No task requires color, three-dimensional vision, or a physical model. Every polygonal specimen is represented by a textual boundary word. Learners unable to manipulate a paper model can complete the entire laboratory using endpoint relations and written quotient data.
