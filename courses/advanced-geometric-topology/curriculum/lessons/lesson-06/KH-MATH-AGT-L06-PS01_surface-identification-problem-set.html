Purpose & Integrity

This independent 30-problem assessment consolidates Lessons 01–05. Work before consulting Lesson 07 solution commentary. A surface name or numerical answer without quotient reasoning is insufficient where justification is requested.

No solutions are embedded in this student file.

Submission Standard

  • State the boundary word and convention when relevant.
  • Record endpoint relations before counting quotient vertices.
  • Count quotient cells, not raw polygon pieces.
  • Distinguish observations, computations, conjectures, benchmark facts, theorem use, and proofs.
  • Use complete sentences for proof/explanation questions.
  • If revising an incorrect argument, preserve a short correction note.

Part A — Foundations

  1. 1. Define an equivalence relation and explain why equivalence classes partition the underlying set.
  2. 2. Let q:X→X/~ be q(x)=[x]. Prove q is surjective.
  3. 3. State the definition of the quotient topology using q and inverse images.
  4. 4. Explain why a polygonal presentation should not be identified literally with its quotient surface.
  5. 5. State the local Euclidean condition used in this course for a surface without boundary.

Part B — Edge and Vertex Audits

  1. 6. For the standard torus word a b a−1 b−1, label the four polygon vertices and derive the endpoint relations. Determine the quotient vertex classes.
  2. 7. For a a, determine V,E,F and χ. Explain which benchmark surface this presentation represents.
  3. 8. For a a−1, determine V,E,F and χ. Compare its vertex-equivalence structure with Problem 7.
  4. 9. For a b a b−1, derive the endpoint relations and determine V,E,F and χ.
  5. 10. Explain why equal numbers of polygon sides do not imply homeomorphic quotient surfaces. Use Problems 7 and 8 as evidence.

Part C — Invariants and Logic

  1. 11. Compute χ for the standard one-square torus quotient from its quotient cell structure.
  2. 12. The torus and Klein bottle both have χ=0 in the standard cell structures studied. Explain why this does not prove they are homeomorphic.
  3. 13. Write the logically valid contrapositive of: “If two finite-CW surfaces are homeomorphic, then they have equal Euler characteristic.”
  4. 14. Explain the difference between an invariant and a complete invariant.
  5. 15. Give a counterexample to the claim: “Closed surfaces with equal Euler characteristic must be homeomorphic,” using only benchmark surfaces already studied.

Part D — Higher-Complexity Presentations

  1. 16. Analyze a b a−1 b−1 c d c−1 d−1. Derive enough endpoint relations to justify V=1, then determine E,F, and χ.
  2. 17. Why is “the polygon has eight sides” not a classification argument for Problem 16?
  3. 18. Compare a a−1 with a b b−1 a−1. Explain what the comparison teaches about presentation length.
  4. 19. Create a four-edge word in which every label appears exactly twice. State your convention and perform a complete quotient-cell audit.
  5. 20. For your word in Problem 19, state precisely what you can conclude using only results established in Lessons 01–05 and what would require a stronger theorem.

Part E — Proof and Forensics

  1. 21. Proof. Prove that all four corners of the standard torus square belong to one equivalence class.
  2. 22. Proof critique. A student writes: “The square has four vertices, four edges, and one face, so χ=1.” Identify the error and repair the argument for the torus.
  3. 23. Proof critique. A student says that a a and a a−1 are equivalent because both pair two edges labeled a. Explain the missing data and why it matters.
  4. 24. Local forensics. An unfamiliar quotient has a point p created by identifying several boundary pieces. Describe a rigorous plan for deciding whether p has a surface neighborhood.
  5. 25. Evidence boundary. You compute χ=−1 for a proper polygonal scheme and find evidence of nonorientability. Write two paragraphs: first, what has actually been established; second, what classification claim would still require an established classification theorem.

Part F — Synthesis

  1. 26. Construct a comparison table for S², RP², T², the Klein bottle, and the orientable genus-two surface containing a benchmark word, V,E,F,χ, and orientability.
  2. 27. From your table, identify one pair separated by Euler characteristic and one pair not separated by Euler characteristic.
  3. 28. Explain why orientability adds information not contained in χ alone.
  4. 29. Write a short algorithm, in your own words, for auditing an unfamiliar polygonal presentation before attempting to name its quotient.
  5. 30. Capstone. Write a 300–500 word mathematical explanation answering: “What information survives the passage from a polygonal presentation to the quotient surface, and how do we know which conclusions are justified?” Use at least two concrete presentations from the course.
