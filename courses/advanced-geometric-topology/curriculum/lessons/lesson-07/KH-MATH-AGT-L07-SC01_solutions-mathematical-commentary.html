How to Use This Lesson

This is not a substitute for attempting Lesson 06. Compare your reasoning, not merely your final answer. When your solution differs, locate the first step at which the mathematical logic diverges.

For proof questions, a correct final statement does not repair a missing argument. For computational questions, correct arithmetic does not repair incorrect quotient-cell counts.

Part A — Foundations

1. Equivalence relations and partitions

An equivalence relation ~ on X is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive. For x∈X, [x]={y∈X:y~x}. The equivalence classes cover X because x∈[x] by reflexivity. If [x] and [y] intersect, choose z in both. Then x~z and z~y (using symmetry as needed), so x~y; transitivity then shows every member of either class belongs to the other. Thus intersecting classes are equal. Hence the classes partition X.

Common error: listing the three axioms without proving the partition claim.

Remediation: revisit Lesson 01's equivalence-relation section and Lesson 02's core definitions.

2. Quotient map

Let C∈X/~. By definition C=[x] for some x∈X. Since q(x)=[x]=C, every element of X/~ has a preimage. Therefore q is surjective.

Proof habit: for surjectivity, begin with an arbitrary element of the codomain.

3. Quotient topology

The quotient topology on X/~ is defined by declaring U⊆X/~ open exactly when q−1(U) is open in X, where q:X→X/~ is the quotient map.

Common error: saying only that “open sets come from X.” The inverse-image criterion is the precise statement.

4. Presentation versus object

The polygonal region is the domain on which identification instructions are recorded. The quotient surface consists of equivalence classes of polygon points. Several polygon points may therefore become one quotient point, and different polygonal presentations may describe homeomorphic quotient spaces.

5. Local condition

Under the convention used in these lessons, a surface without boundary must be locally Euclidean of dimension two: each point has a neighborhood homeomorphic to an open subset of R² (equivalently, after suitable shrinking, an open disk).

Part B — Edge and Vertex Audits

6. Standard torus

For a b a−1 b−1, label the square vertices v0,v1,v2,v3 in boundary order. Under the convention used in Lessons 02–06, the a-pair gives v0~v3 and v1~v2; the b-pair gives v1~v0 and v2~v3. Transitivity yields v0~v1~v2~v3. Thus there is one quotient vertex class.

7. The word a a

The same-direction pair identifies the two polygon vertices, so V=1. There is one edge class and one face, hence E=1 and F=1. Therefore χ=1−1+1=1. This is the benchmark presentation of RP² used in the course.

8. The word a a−1

Here the endpoint correspondence leaves two vertex classes. Thus V=2, E=1, F=1 and χ=2. This is the benchmark 2-gon presentation of S². The comparison with Problem 7 demonstrates that pairing labels alone are insufficient; orientation/correspondence data changes the quotient.

9. Klein-bottle word

For a b a b−1, the a-pair and b-pair together identify all four square vertices. Hence V=1, E=2, F=1 and χ=0. This is the benchmark Klein-bottle presentation used in the course.

10. Same number of sides

Problems 7 and 8 both begin with a 2-gon, yet they produce different endpoint equivalence structures, different Euler characteristics, and different benchmark surfaces. The number of sides of the presentation is therefore not sufficient to determine the quotient's homeomorphism type.

Part C — Invariants and Logic

11. Torus Euler characteristic

After quotienting, the standard square torus cell structure has V=1, E=2, F=1. Therefore χ=1−2+1=0.

12. Equal χ is insufficient

The implication “homeomorphic ⇒ same Euler characteristic” does not imply its converse. The torus and Klein bottle both have χ=0, while the former is orientable and the latter is nonorientable. Thus χ alone does not distinguish them.

13. Contrapositive

If two such surfaces have different Euler characteristics, then they are not homeomorphic.

Logic note: this is the contrapositive of the invariant statement, not its converse.

14. Invariant versus complete invariant

An invariant takes the same value on homeomorphic objects. A complete invariant, for the class under study, also distinguishes homeomorphism types: equality of the complete invariant is sufficient for equivalence. Euler characteristic is an invariant but is not by itself a complete invariant for the closed surfaces encountered here.

15. Counterexample

T² and the Klein bottle provide the required counterexample: both have χ=0, but orientability distinguishes them. Therefore equal Euler characteristic does not imply homeomorphism.

Part D — Higher-Complexity Presentations

16. Orientable genus-two presentation

For a b a−1 b−1 c d c−1 d−1, the standard endpoint identifications place all eight polygon vertices into one equivalence class. The four labels give four edge classes and the polygon interior gives one face. Thus V=1, E=4, F=1 and χ=1−4+1=−2.

Required method: the answer must arise from quotient-cell analysis, not from substituting genus into a formula that was excluded by the problem.

17. Why “eight sides” is not classification

Side count belongs to the chosen presentation, and presentation complexity is not a homeomorphism invariant. Identifications determine the quotient structure.

18. Expanded sphere presentation

The words a a−1 and a b b−1 a−1 illustrate that an elementary adjacent inverse-pair simplification can enlarge a presentation without changing the represented surface. Therefore boundary-word length is not a topological invariant.

19. Student-created word

Answers vary. A complete response must state a convention, use each label exactly twice, derive endpoint relations, form vertex classes, count quotient edges/faces, and compute χ. A surface name is optional unless justified by established results.

20. Limits of inference

A valid answer reports the verified quotient data and any established properties. It must not silently use the full surface-classification theorem before that theorem enters the curriculum. Stronger classification requires an already proved theorem or a direct homeomorphism argument.

Part E — Proof and Forensics

21. One torus vertex class

From the a-identification obtain v0~v3 and v1~v2. From the b-identification obtain v1~v0 and v2~v3. Since v1~v0 and v0~v3, transitivity gives v1~v3; together with the remaining relations all four vertices lie in one class. Therefore the quotient cell structure has V=1.

22. Faulty raw-polygon count

The error is counting the square's cells before identification. In the torus quotient, four corners become one vertex class and four sides become two edge classes; the interior remains one face. Therefore χ=1−2+1=0.

23. Missing orientation data

The label a identifies the pair, but it does not by itself specify which endpoint corresponds to which. The words a a and a a−1 encode different directional relationships under the course convention. Their induced endpoint equivalence classes differ, so the quotient spaces differ.

24. Local forensics

Let p be the suspicious quotient point. Determine q−1(p), choose sufficiently small neighborhoods of those preimage points, and analyze how the quotient identification joins those neighborhood pieces. To be a surface point under the current convention, p must have a neighborhood homeomorphic to an open subset of R². Branching, pinching, or other non-disk-like local structure demonstrates failure of the surface condition.

25. Evidence boundary

Established: assuming the cell audit is correct, χ=−1. The identification data also supplies stated evidence of nonorientability if that property has been justified by methods already established.

Not yet automatically established: a unique homeomorphism classification derived from the pair (χ, orientability). Making that step generally invokes the classification of compact connected surfaces or another sufficient result. Until such a theorem is available, report the invariants without pretending they constitute a complete proof of the name.

27. Separated / not separated

S² and T² are separated by Euler characteristic because 2≠0. T² and the Klein bottle are not separated by Euler characteristic because both have χ=0.

28. Orientability adds information

Euler characteristic records one numerical topological invariant. Orientability records whether a consistent local orientation can be chosen globally. Since T² and the Klein bottle share χ but differ in orientability, orientability contains information not captured by χ alone.

29. Audit algorithm

State the convention; identify edge pairs and directions; derive endpoint relations; take transitive closure; count quotient vertices, edges, and faces; compute χ; inspect orientation behavior; check local Euclidean neighborhoods when necessary; then state only the classification conclusion supported by established results.

30. Capstone commentary

Answers vary. A strong response explains that raw polygon geometry is presentation-dependent, while the quotient construction produces topological data through equivalence classes. Strong responses use concrete examples, explain the limits of Euler characteristic, and explicitly separate what was observed in a representation from what was proved about the quotient.
