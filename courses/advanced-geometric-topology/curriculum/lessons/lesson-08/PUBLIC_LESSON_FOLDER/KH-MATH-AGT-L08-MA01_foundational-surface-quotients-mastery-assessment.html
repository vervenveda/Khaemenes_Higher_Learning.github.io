Foundational Surface Quotients — Mastery Assessment

Advanced Geometric Topology · Self-Paced Lesson 08

Resource ID: KH-MATH-AGT-L08-MA01

Mastery threshold: 80% plus resolution of all critical-concept errors

Purpose

This assessment measures transfer from Lessons 01–07. Most tasks use new wording, new combinations of ideas, or unfamiliar presentations. The goal is to determine whether you can perform quotient-space reasoning independently.

Assessment Rules

  • Show reasoning for every nontrivial conclusion.
  • When analyzing a polygonal word, state your convention.
  • Derive endpoint relations before asserting quotient vertex counts.
  • Do not use the full classification theorem unless a problem explicitly permits it.
  • A diagram may support an argument but may not replace written quotient data.
  • The Khaemenes Scientific Calculator is unnecessary and should not replace reasoning.

Section I — Precision Check (15 points)

1. Quotient construction — 5 points

Let X be a topological space and ~ an equivalence relation on X. Define X/~, the quotient map q, and the quotient topology. Your definition must explain exactly how openness in X/~ is determined.

2. Equivalence logic — 5 points

Suppose x~y and y~z. State which axiom implies x~z. Then explain why equivalence classes that intersect must be equal.

3. Presentation discipline — 5 points

Explain, in 3–5 sentences, why the number of sides of a polygonal presentation is not itself a topological invariant of the quotient surface.

Section II — Unseen Quotient Audits (30 points)

4. Presentation A — 10 points

Analyze the 6-gon word a b c a−1 b−1 c−1. Label the six vertices v0,…,v5 in boundary order; derive endpoint relations for each paired label; find all vertex equivalence classes; determine V,E,F; compute χ; and state what these results do and do not prove using only results established so far.

5. Presentation B — 10 points

Analyze the 4-gon word a b a−1 b. Derive endpoint relations; find the vertex equivalence classes; determine V,E,F and χ; identify orientation-reversing evidence in the edge data; and do not name the surface unless your conclusion is justified by an established benchmark or argument.

6. Compare A and B — 10 points

Compare the two quotient audits. Identify at least three mathematically meaningful differences or similarities. Explain why a single shared numerical quantity, if one occurs, would not by itself prove homeomorphism.

Section III — Invariant Logic (15 points)

7. One-way reasoning — 5 points

A topological invariant I satisfies: if X≅Y, then I(X)=I(Y). Write the contrapositive. Then explain why the converse need not hold.

8. Counterexample — 5 points

Give two benchmark closed surfaces from Lessons 01–07 that have equal Euler characteristic but are not homeomorphic. State the additional property already studied that distinguishes them.

9. Error diagnosis — 5 points

A learner says: “My quotient has χ=2, so it must be a sphere.” Diagnose the logical gap without assuming the classification theorem.

Section IV — Proof & Local Topology (20 points)

10. Proof — 10 points

Let q:X→X/~ be the quotient map. Prove that if A⊆X/~ is open, then q−1(A) is open in X. Be explicit about which definition supplies the conclusion. Then explain why this statement alone is not a proof that q is an open map.

11. Local forensic problem — 10 points

A quotient construction creates a point p from several boundary pieces. Away from p, every point visibly has a disk-like neighborhood. Give a rigorous investigation plan for p. Identify the relevant preimage data and the local condition required before the entire quotient may be called a surface without boundary.

Section V — Synthesis & Transfer (20 points)

12. New specimen — 10 points

Design a polygonal presentation using either 6 or 8 edges, with every edge label appearing exactly twice. Do not simply copy a benchmark word. Provide the word and convention; endpoint relations; vertex classes; V,E,F and χ; orientation evidence; and a statement separating what you computed from what you proved.

13. Mathematical reflection — 10 points

In 250–400 words, answer: Why is quotient reasoning more reliable than identifying a surface by visual resemblance? Use at least two concrete mathematical ideas from the course, such as equivalence classes, local Euclidean neighborhoods, Euler characteristic, orientability, or nonunique polygonal presentations.

Critical Concepts

A score of 80 or higher is necessary but not sufficient for mastery. The following unresolved errors require remediation and reassessment:

  • counting raw polygon corners/edges instead of quotient classes;
  • ignoring edge-orientation correspondence;
  • confusing equal invariant values with proof of homeomorphism;
  • treating a picture as sufficient proof;
  • calling an arbitrary quotient a surface without the required local check;
  • using an unestablished classification theorem to supply a conclusion.

Mastery Decision

Mastered: ≥80/100 and no unresolved critical-concept error.

Revision required: ≥80/100 with a critical-concept error. Complete targeted remediation and reassess that competency.

Not yet mastered: <80/100. Use the remediation map and complete a new assessment form after review.

A self-paced learner may revise and reassess. The objective is demonstrated mathematical mastery.
