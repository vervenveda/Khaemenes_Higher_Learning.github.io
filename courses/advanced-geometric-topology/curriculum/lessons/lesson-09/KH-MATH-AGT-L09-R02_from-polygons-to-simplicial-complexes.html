Arc 02: Triangulations & Combinatorial Surfaces

Why We Are Changing Languages

Lessons 01–08 described surfaces using polygons and quotient identifications. That language is powerful, but topology also needs a systematic way to break spaces into elementary pieces that meet along well-controlled faces. Simplicial complexes provide that language.

polygon + gluing instructions → triangles and faces → combinatorial complex → topological realization

1. Simplices

A 0-simplex is a vertex, a 1-simplex is an edge, a 2-simplex is a triangle, and a 3-simplex is a tetrahedron. More generally, a k-simplex has k+1 vertices.

For an abstract simplex with vertices v0,…,vk, every nonempty subset of those vertices determines a face. Thus a triangle [a,b,c] carries the edges [a,b], [b,c], [a,c] and the vertices [a], [b], [c].

2. Abstract Simplicial Complexes

An abstract simplicial complex K is a collection of finite nonempty vertex sets such that whenever σ belongs to K, every nonempty subset of σ also belongs to K.

This face-closure condition is essential.

Example

If {a,b,c}∈K, then K must also contain {a,b}, {a,c}, {b,c}, {a}, {b}, and {c}.

Nonexample

The collection {{a,b,c},{a},{b},{c}} is not a simplicial complex because the edges of the triangle are missing.

Convention note: Some texts include the empty simplex in every abstract simplicial complex. This course uses the nonempty-face convention unless a later resource explicitly says otherwise.

3. Dimension

A simplex containing k+1 vertices has dimension k. The dimension of a finite simplicial complex is the maximum dimension of one of its simplices.

4. Geometric Realization

An abstract simplicial complex records incidence combinatorially. A geometric realization |K| turns this data into a topological space by realizing the abstract simplices geometrically and joining them according to their common faces.

The combinatorial object K and its realized topological space |K| are related but conceptually distinct—much as a polygonal presentation and its quotient surface were distinct in Arc 01.

5. How Simplices Are Allowed to Meet

In a geometric simplicial complex, simplices meet along common faces. Two filled triangles may share an entire edge, share a vertex, or be disjoint. They may not cross through one another in an uncontrolled way and still count as a geometric simplicial complex in that realization.

This controlled-intersection rule is what makes combinatorial incidence faithfully describe the topology.

6. A First 2-Dimensional Complex

Let K contain the two maximal simplices {a,b,c} and {a,c,d}, together with all of their faces.

The triangles share the edge {a,c}. The complex has:

  • V=4 vertices;
  • E=5 edges: ab,bc,ac,cd,ad;
  • F=2 triangles.

χ=V−E+F=4−5+2=1.

Do not classify the realization merely from this number. First ask what kind of space has actually been constructed and whether boundary is present.

7. Triangulations

A triangulation of a topological space X is, in the language used here, a simplicial complex K together with a homeomorphism |K|→X.

The triangles are not merely drawn on X for decoration. Their realization must reproduce X topologically.

A single space can admit many different triangulations. The triangulation is structure placed on the space; it is not the space's unique identity.

8. From Our Square Torus to Triangles

In Arc 01, the torus was represented by a square with opposite edges identified. Drawing a diagonal divides that square into two triangles and gives an efficient Δ-complex description after the quotient.

This two-triangle torus model is not an ordinary simplicial complex: the quotient identifications allow incidences that violate the stricter condition that an abstract simplex is determined by its vertex set and that geometric simplices meet as ordinary simplicial-complex faces.

Lesson: “built from triangles” does not automatically mean “simplicial complex.” We must know the precise combinatorial category being used.

10. Faces, Facets, and the f-Vector

A face is any simplex contained in the complex. A facet is a simplex maximal under inclusion. For a finite 2-dimensional complex, the f-vector is commonly written (f0,f1,f2), where f0 counts vertices, f1 edges, and f2 triangles.

For the two-triangle example above, the f-vector is (4,5,2), and χ=f0−f1+f2=1.

This notation prepares us to generalize Euler characteristic beyond one polygonal presentation.

11. Boundary Begins to Appear Combinatorially

For a triangulated 2-manifold, an interior edge is incident to two triangles, while a boundary edge is incident to one triangle. This provides a first combinatorial signal of boundary.

Incidence counts alone are not the whole local-manifold test. At vertices we must inspect how neighboring simplices fit together. Lesson 10 formalizes this using stars and links.

12. A Combinatorial Audit

Given a finite 2-dimensional simplicial complex:

  1. List the maximal simplices.
  2. Verify face closure.
  3. List all vertices, edges, and triangles without duplication.
  4. Compute the f-vector.
  5. Compute χ=f0−f1+f2.
  6. Count how many triangles meet each edge.
  7. Flag boundary-like and nonmanifold-like behavior.
  8. Inspect vertex neighborhoods before claiming the realization is a surface.

13. Worked Audit

Let the facets be abc, acd, ade, and aeb.

The vertex set is {a,b,c,d,e}. The distinct edges are ab,bc,ac,cd,ad,de,ae,eb. Thus

f=(5,8,4), χ=5−8+4=1.

Now inspect edge incidences. Edges ab, ac, ad, and ae each occur in two triangles, while bc, cd, de, and eb occur once. The complex therefore exhibits boundary edges. This already tells us that it is not a closed triangulated surface.

Whether it is a surface with boundary still requires the corresponding local checks at vertices.

14. A Nonmanifold Warning

Suppose three distinct triangles all share the same edge ab. A point in the interior of ab then has three triangular sheets meeting there. Its neighborhood is not locally a disk or half-disk. Thus the realization fails to be a 2-manifold at such points.

This is the simplicial analogue of the local-forensics discipline developed in Arc 01.

15. Why This Language Matters

Simplicial complexes let topology become partly combinatorial. Questions about spaces can be translated into questions about vertices, edges, faces, incidence, chains, and maps. This language will later support simplicial homology and other algebraic invariants.

The goal is not to replace geometry. It is to encode enough geometry combinatorially that rigorous calculations become possible.

Knowledge Check

  1. What closure property defines an abstract simplicial complex?
  2. Why must a triangle bring all of its edges and vertices with it?
  3. What is the dimension of a simplex with six vertices?
  4. Distinguish K from |K|.
  5. What does it mean for K to triangulate X?
  6. Why is the two-triangle square model of the torus naturally a Δ-complex rather than an ordinary simplicial complex?
  7. Define the f-vector of a finite 2-dimensional complex.
  8. What edge-incidence pattern suggests boundary?
  9. Why do three triangles sharing one edge create a local manifold problem?
  10. Why is Euler characteristic still evidence rather than a complete classification argument?

Independent Mini-Investigation

Create a finite 2-dimensional abstract simplicial complex with at least three triangles.

  1. List its facets.
  2. Generate all required faces.
  3. Compute its f-vector and Euler characteristic.
  4. Record the number of incident triangles for every edge.
  5. Identify any apparent boundary edges.
  6. State what additional local information you would need before calling |K| a surface.
