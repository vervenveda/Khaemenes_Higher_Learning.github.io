Rationalization and Radical Limits

A limit describes the output behavior produced by inputs approaching a target. The value at the target may agree with the limit, differ from it, or be undefined. Numerical, graphical, and algebraic evidence should be reconciled. This lesson emphasizes rationalization and radical limits through symbolic, graphical, numerical, and contextual evidence.

Essential question

How can values near an input reveal behavior at that input—even when direct substitution fails?

Visual model

A limit describes the output behavior produced by inputs approaching a target. The value at the target may agree with the limit, differ from it, or be undefined. Numerical, graphical, and algebraic evidence should be reconciled.

\lim_{x\to a}f(x)=L\lim_{x\to a^-}f(x)\lim_{x\to a^+}f(x)

Worked reasoning

Table-to-limit reasoningEvaluate inputs on both sides of the target, watch whether the outputs settle near one number, and report one-sided behavior.
Algebraic reasoningUse direct substitution first. When it gives an indeterminate form, transform the expression without changing nearby values.

Misconception check: A limit is not found by merely substituting the target. Substitution works only when the function's local structure permits it.

Answer: No
Reasoning: The one-sided limits must agree.

Answer: 1
Reasoning: This fundamental trigonometric limit equals 1 in radians.
